An ADHD-Friendly To-Do List: 7 Rules That Actually Stick

September 17, 2026 · 8 min read · by the Cleartable team

Most productivity advice assumes a brain that remembers what it decided this morning, feels the difference between urgent and important, and starts a task simply because it's time to start. If that isn't how your brain works, whether you have an ADHD diagnosis, suspect one, or just recognise the pattern, then most to-do lists are built for someone else.

This isn't medical advice, and no app replaces support from a clinician if you want one. But a lot of people with distractible, novelty-hungry, time-blind brains have found that a few structural rules make a to-do list go from "another thing I abandoned" to "the one thing that holds." Here are the seven that come up again and again.

1. One inbox, and it has to be effortless

The moment a thought arrives ("renew the passport", "reply to Dana", "that idea for the talk") you have about four seconds before it's gone. If capturing it takes six taps and a decision about which project it belongs to, it will not get captured. It will get lost, and then it will resurface at 2am.

So: one place, always open, zero decisions at capture time. Type the thought in plain words and move on. Sorting is a separate job for a separate moment. The best version of this is an app that lets you type "passport renewal before the Lisbon trip in November" and quietly files the date and the project itself, so the four-second window is enough.

2. Write the next physical action, not the project

"Sort out taxes" is not a task. It's a fog. Fog doesn't have a first step, so a brain that needs a clear starting point will bounce off it every time it looks. "Find last year's return in the Downloads folder" is a task: you can see yourself doing it, and it takes four minutes.

Every item on the list should be something you could start in the next sixty seconds if you had to. If it isn't, break it until it is. This one rule removes more procrastination than any amount of motivation.

3. Make time visible

Time blindness is real: "later today" feels the same at 9am and 4pm, and a two-hour task and a ten-minute task look identical on a list. The fix is to put tasks where time is visible: on a calendar, next to the meetings. Seeing "call the bank" sitting in a 30-minute gap before lunch makes it concrete in a way a bullet point never will. The list says what. Only the calendar says when, and when is the part a distractible brain needs help with.

4. Today is a short list, and it's the only list you look at

A forty-item master list is a wall of guilt. Looking at it feels bad, so you stop looking at it, so it stops working. Keep the master list, but never work from it. Each morning (or the night before) pull three to five items into a Today view and look only at that. Everything else is out of sight, safely stored, not shouting. If the app can hide tasks until their start date, use that ruthlessly.

5. Externalise the deciding

Every "what should I do next?" costs attention you don't have to spare, and by mid-afternoon the answer is always "check my phone." Decide once, in the morning, what the order is, and then just follow it. Better still, let something else decide the order for you: a rule (shortest first), a time block, or an AI that lays the day out and asks you to approve it. Following a plan is far easier than making fifty small decisions under fatigue.

6. Build in forgiveness

You will miss days. A system that punishes a missed day with a broken streak, a red overdue badge, or a pile of rolled-over shame will be abandoned within a fortnight, because who wants to open the thing that scolds them? Look for tools where a missed habit can be marked as a rest day without resetting anything, where overdue tasks quietly move forward rather than turning red, and where "today" starts clean. Consistency comes from returning, not from never leaving.

7. Keep the novelty somewhere it can't hurt you

Novelty is fuel, and switching apps every three weeks is how a lot of people burn it. Instead of restarting the system, redirect the itch: change the order of your Today list, try a new context tag, run a brain dump into the inbox. A system that lets you fiddle with it a little is one you won't feel the need to replace entirely.

The bottom line

The goal is not to become a person who loves lists. It's to build a list that needs less of you: less memory, less prioritising, less willpower at the moment of starting. One inbox, tiny next steps, visible time, a short Today, decisions made in advance, forgiveness for the misses, and a little room to fiddle. That's a system a distractible brain can actually keep.

Frequently asked questions

What makes a to-do list ADHD-friendly?

Low friction at capture (one inbox, plain words, no sorting decisions in the moment), tasks written as concrete next actions, time made visible on a calendar, a short Today view with everything else hidden, and forgiveness for missed days instead of streaks and red badges.

Should I use a paper list or an app if I have ADHD?

Whichever you will actually open. Paper is fast and tactile but can't hide future tasks or put things on a calendar. An app works better if it lets you capture in plain language, hides tasks until their start date, and sits next to your calendar. Many people use paper for the morning three and an app as the memory behind it.

Can AI help with ADHD task management?

It can take over the parts that cost the most attention: filing a captured thought into the right project and date, breaking a fuzzy item into a first step, and laying out the day around existing meetings. The useful version proposes and asks for approval rather than silently rearranging things.
